The mouth of the guitar: the portal of the voice

The soundhole of the guitar, located in the center of the soundboard and surrounded by the rosette, is much more than an opening: it is the portal through which the sound is projected and its acoustic character is defined.

Workshop Mariano Conde

The soundhole of the guitar, that circular opening in the center of the soundboard, guarded by the rosette as if it were a carefully crafted frame, is much more than a simple window through which the sound comes out. Anyone who has spent years among wood, varnish and the silent dust of a workshop knows that around the soundhole of the guitar there is a magic, a science and a heritage that is respected. In this article, as heir to a legacy of generations, I will tell in depth the role, technique, evolution and meaning of the mouth in theflamenco guitarandclassic, following the pulse of those of us who have learned to let the wood speak.

The meeting point: The physical and symbolic location

In the design of the Spanish guitar, the soundhole is not just an opening into the void. It is located in the exact center of the soundboard, in the very heart of the sound box. Its position, symmetrical and strategic, responds to centuries of observation and trial-error; It allows the vibrations that are born in the bridge and travel through the top to have their natural exit point, that moment in which the contained air is released and the sound acquires character, body and projection.

For those of us who have grown up listening to, and tuning, every millimeter of the instrument, the mouth is the threshold where mechanics and art meet and recognize each other; where the unmistakable voice of each guitar truly begins.

Acoustic function: Opening to sound

To talk about the mouth is to talk, above all, about sound. The circular opening plays the primary role of allowing the pressure waves, generated by the vibration of the strings and amplified by the case, to be projected out into the world. Without the mouth, the guitar would be a mute drum, a resonant box without a voice. It is here where the sound takes direction, timbre and presence.

The proportions of the mouth, its diameter and its integration into the lid are the result of a careful balance: too large, and the lid loses rigidity and vibration capacity; too small, and the sound remains trapped, deprived of freedom. Every luthier knows well that, in that margin of a few millimeters, it is decided whether the guitar will be bright, dark, dry or round. The mouth, then, is the silent regulator of sound breathing.

The rosette: Guardian and ornament

Around the mouth rests the rosette, that minutely ornamented ring that has been an object of art and mystery throughout history. In our workshop, the rosette not only embellishes: it reinforces the structure of the lid, protects the edge of the soundhole from wear and possible cracks and provides identity to the instrument, like a subtle signature of the person who builds it.

Each rosette is made up of infinite fragments of wood, meticulously assembled mosaics, feathers and fillets that reflect the taste, tradition and history of each guitar house. The rosette is not just aesthetic; It is a seal of respect for the material and the craft.

Materials and techniques: Inheritance and experimentation

The perimeter of the mouth must be perfectly clean and polished; Any irregularity can affect the response of the soundboard and, by extension, the overall tone of the instrument. For this reason, the choice of tools, the type of cut and the finish of the mouth are monitored down to the last detail.

In the tradition of our ancestors, the soundhole is reinforced internally by placing small crossbars or braces, following patterns that depend on the character sought: more tension for concert guitars, more lightness and immediate response for flamenco guitars. The integration with the soundboard and the choice of woods determine the strength, elasticity and resonance of the set.

The influence of the mouth on the character of the instrument

A slight change in the diameter of the soundhole, usually 80 to 88 mm on classical and flamenco guitars, can profoundly modify the personality of the instrument. A wider mouth enhances projection and brightness, while a smaller one concentrates the sound, giving it greater depth and sweetness. This adjustment, often imperceptible to the untrained eye, is decisive in achieving the perfect balance between volume, projection and nuance.

The relationship between the mouth and the arrangement of the trusses on the cover is another invisible art. The braces should surround and reinforce the mouth without limiting the vibration of the lid. In flamenco, we prefer immediacy of response and clarity, so the reinforcement around the mouth is worked with the minimum possible material, trusting in the luthier's skill rather than excess support.

Mouth, air and box: the secret triangle

The dialogue between the mouth, the volume of thesoundboardand the quality of the woods used determines the acoustic essence of the guitar. The amount of air that enters and leaves through the mouth activates and modulates certain frequencies, a kind of “natural filter” that enhances or attenuates harmonics depending on the geometry and materials.

Here is the mystery of the instrument: two guitars made with the same woods but different soundholes do not sound the same. The volume and emotional balance of the guitar rest on this exact point of communion between science and art.

The mouth as a work of identity

For a builder, each mouth is a declaration of intent. The care of the circle, the perfection of the cut, the accuracy of the diameter and the quality of the finish speak of the seriousness and passion with which the instrument has been conceived. The ancients said that you could judge the guitar player by the cleanliness of the soundhole and the quality of the rosette.

In our workshop, each guitar has a soundhole that embodies respect for the school and the desire to leave a unique mark on each instrument that leaves our hands. The mouth not only lets out the guitar's voice; It also represents the silence of the builder, his pulse and his faith in that which, once finished, no longer belongs to him.

Evolution and tradition: From history to the present

If the shape of the mouth has remained almost unchanged since the 19th century, it is because it fulfills its mission with the effectiveness of the essential. The real revolutions have been details: new mosaics in the rosette, slight changes in diameter adapted to the demands of concert artists or the whims of pure flamenco, some modern attempts at oval or displaced mouths that, over time, have once again surrendered to the perfection of the circle.

The mouth is the unalterable echo of a timeline that unites generations, the visible mark of an evolution that never forgets its roots.

The guitarist and the mouth: A constant dialogue

For the performer, the mouth is the ultimate border between wood and air, the point from which the guitar responds to the energy and intentions of the touch. Changing the angle, moving closer or further away from the mouth with the right hand, searching for sounds near the bridge or towards the fretboard: everything is a game of possibilities that the mouth, in its humble opening, powers and reveals.

Listening carefully to the breathing of the guitar is, deep down, listening to the secret ear of the mouth, that natural filter that decides which part of the soul of the wood reaches the listener and which remains, resonating in the intimacy of the instrument.

Conclusion

To talk about the soundhole on the guitar is to talk about the birth of the sound and the ultimate goal of the guitarist-luthier's work. It is entrusting a circle of a few centimeters with the mission of transforming pulsation into emotion, wood into art, effort into resonance. For those who, like us, see each guitar as a unique creature, the soundhole is not only a constructive detail, but a profound symbol of our way of understanding the craft. In each mouth we leave the mark of our workshop, the humble testimony of an inherited passion. Thus, when the guitar begins to play and the music crosses that circular threshold, we know that the voice that comes out is, at the same time, the voice of the guitarist and that of all of us who, generation after generation, have believed in the miracle of wood turned into soul.

Mariano Conde
