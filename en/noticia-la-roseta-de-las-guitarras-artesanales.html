The rosette of handmade guitars

The rosette of the Mariano Conde guitars is a unique and handmade work. Discover its history, meaning and the process that makes each guitar unique.

Workshop Mariano Conde

The rosette of a guitar is one of the most emblematic and distinctive elements of the instrument, both aesthetically and handcrafted. In Mariano Conde's workshop, the rosette is never simply a decoration; It is the reflection of the soul of each guitar and, above all, of the philosophy that has guided our guitar family for generations: each guitar should be unique, just like the rosette that adorns it. Unless the client expressly wishes, each design is born new, never repeated, because we understand that each instrument is unrepeatable, destined to transmit different feelings and nuances.

Meaning and function of the rosette

In the Spanish guitar, the rosette is the decoration that surrounds the soundhole, that circular opening through which the sound is projected and finds its place in the concert hall, in the studio or between the hands of the performer. Although the function of the soundhole is mainly acoustic, allowing the projection and expansion of sound from the soundboard, the rosette fulfills an almost liturgical function: it is the meeting point between visual art and sound art, the indisputable border between luthiery as a technique and the guitar as a work of art. Its presence turns the guitar into a jewel. You can learn more about the parts of the guitar in thisdetail of the parts of a classical guitar.

History and tradition

The rosette has been a fundamental part of the Spanish guitar since time immemorial. Often inspired by Mudejar, geometric and floral motifs, the artisanal tradition of its production involves meticulous mosaic, fillet and feather techniques. At Mariano Conde, we preserve these techniques because we understand that they are part of the legacy that defines classical Spanish guitar, but each rosette that comes out of our hands is reinvention, not repetition.

Modern and even digital interpretations of the rosette have emerged over the years, but respect for painstaking work and attention to detail has always remained. The rosette can be composed of thousands of small wooden pieces, sometimes inspired by historical ceramic motifs, other times by simple impressions of nature, always proposed with the seal of artisan creativity.

Exclusivity: a rosette, a guitar, a story

My motto as a luthier is to never repeat the same rosette design twice, unless the client insists on an exact reproduction of a guitar displayed on our website or on a social network. That is the only exception. Everything else is governed by the need and pleasure of creating something new, of turning each instrument into a personal, non-transferable object for life.

Inspiration can come from anywhere: an old pottery, a painting, traditional embroidery or even a simple sketch on paper. Before cutting the first piece of wood, I draw and conceptualize the design, always looking for a balance between tradition and something that has not been done yet. It satisfies me to know that, when a musician holds a Mariano Conde guitar, he holds something unique, unrepeatable and imbued with soul. The artisanal production process

The process of creating a rosette begins with the selection of noble and fine woods: maple, ebony, rosewood, sometimes combined with less conventional materials or with subtle and natural color dyes. Then comes the craftsmanship: we cut, arrange and assemble thousands of tiny pieces of wood, ensuring that each fragment fits and stands out within the overall pattern we have conceived for that particular instrument.

The encapsulation of these pieces requires, above all, skill and patience. The design is built into a template, and then delicately inlaid into the soundboard, surrounding the soundhole of the guitar. The objective is for the rosette to serve at the same time as the personal seal of the workshop and as a fingerprint of the instrument. If the work is perfect, the rosette will be perfectly flush with the wood of the top: it will look and feel an intrinsic part of the guitar, not a simple addition.

The relationship with the client

One of the greatest attractions of my job is the direct relationship with the guitarist. There are those who arrive with a clear idea, with a saved photograph of a rosette that they saw in the workshop or even at a concert. In those cases, the challenge is to recreate that design with the same care that the original artisan put into it. But I insist: unless expressly requested, the seal of authenticity is avoiding repetition.

For me, the guitar—and the rosette particularly—is the hallmark of the musician. Therefore, I listen to each suggestion, analyze the style, personality, and even the repertoire that the future owner of the instrument usually performs. Everything serves to shape the visual “personality” of the rosette. Sometimes the design may include personal motifs, symbols or preferred colors, tiny details that only the performer will recognize.

Technique and aesthetics: the functional and the beautiful

Despite its ornamental status, the rosette fulfills a primary technical function: it protects the edges of the soundhole of the guitar, preventing fissures or cracks from forming due to impacts or changes in humidity. However, its specific weight is aesthetic and emotional, since the rosette becomes the true calling card of any guitar. The combination of woods, the colors and the arrangement of the mosaic are integrated until the guitar, as a whole, transmits visual harmony and stylistic coherence with the rest of the handcrafted details: the headstock, the threading of the sides, the nut...

Many times, the first emotional bond that the future owner establishes with his guitar is, precisely, with the design of the rosette. An artist knows immediately if that geometric motif, that nod to the Mudejar, that contrast of colors or that discreet elegance of light and dark woods speak directly to the heart.

Conclusion: dynamic tradition and the art of not repeating

The rosette, more than a simple ornament, is the best example of the unmistakable personality of each Mariano Conde guitar. It is tradition, but also innovation. It is respect for the past and incessant search for the new. Every time I design a rosette, it is like starting over from scratch: creating an unrepeatable microcosm, a visual signature that no one will be able to find twice unless the musician decides so. Because in the guitar, as in life itself, each story deserves a unique framework, and no two souls are the same: nor two guitars, nor two rosettes.

Mariano Conde
